# C12: Monitoring, Logging & Anomaly Detection

> **Source:** [`1.01-dev/en/0x10-C12-Monitoring-and-Logging.md`](https://github.com/OWASP/AISVS/blob/main/1.01-dev/en/0x10-C12-Monitoring-and-Logging.md)
> **Requirements:** 21 | **Sections:** 5

## Control Objective

Deliver real-time and forensic visibility into what the model and other AI components see, do, and return, so AI-specific threats can be detected, triaged, and learned from. The chapter scope is deliberately AI-specific: log content unique to AI systems (model identifier, token usage, safety filter outcomes, prompt/response handling), AI-specific abuse and attack detection (jailbreak, prompt injection, extraction, multi-turn trajectory, covert channels over LLM endpoints), model and data drift detection, AI telemetry signals (token attribution, output/input ratio anomalies), proactive agent behavior monitoring, and training-data and model-lifecycle audit logging.

## Threat Landscape

Known attacks, real-world incidents, and threat vectors relevant to this chapter:

- **Shadow AI and unmonitored usage** — Varonis's 2025 State of Data Security Report (1,000 organizations) found that 98% have employees using unsanctioned apps, including shadow AI, with an average of 1,200 unofficial apps per company; both figures cover unsanctioned apps generally, not AI alone. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report (600 breached organizations) found that breaches involving shadow AI incidents averaged USD 4.63M and took 247 days to identify and contain, against 241 days overall, and that organizations with high levels of shadow AI averaged USD 670K more per breach than those with low levels or none.
- **AI-specific failure detection gaps** — Traditional metrics (latency, error rates) can appear healthy while output quality silently degrades (LangChain's observability guide). In LangChain's State of Agent Engineering survey (1,300+ respondents), 62% report detailed agent tracing, and about 72% of respondents with agents in production report full tracing. In Elastic's 2026 observability survey (500+ IT decision-makers), 85% of respondents already using GenAI for observability plan LLM observability, but 8% had finished implementing it.
- **Multi-turn abuse trajectories** — Crescendo attacks (USENIX Security 2025) escalate through benign-seeming turns; Deceptive Delight (Palo Alto Unit 42) reached an average 65% attack success rate within three turns across eight models and 8,000 test cases. Per-request monitoring can miss these cross-turn patterns.
- **Log tampering and forensic integrity** — PoisonedRAG reported a 90% attack success rate on targeted questions after injecting five malicious texts per question into a knowledge base of millions of texts. In a simulated attack on The Pile, replacing 0.001% of training tokens with medical misinformation produced models (1.3B and 4B parameters) more likely to propagate medical errors that still matched clean models on common open-source medical benchmarks (Nature Medicine, 2025). Beyond data poisoning, AI agent logs are themselves attack targets — agents that make autonomous decisions without reliable logging create blind spots that attackers exploit to hide data exfiltration or unauthorized actions. Adversaries may also spoof agent identities in multi-agent systems to perform actions under another persona.
- **AI-assisted attack operations** — GTG-1002 campaign (disclosed by Anthropic in November 2025; activity detected in mid-September 2025) targeted roughly 30 organizations. Anthropic reported that the threat actor used AI to perform 80-90% of the campaign, including reconnaissance, exploit development, and lateral movement, with human intervention at perhaps 4-6 critical decision points per campaign (vendor-reported).
- **Cascading failures in agentic systems** — OWASP's Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026) dedicated ASI08 specifically to cascading failures. Memory injection attacks (Lakera AI research) demonstrate how indirect prompt injection via poisoned data sources can corrupt an agent's long-term memory, creating persistent false beliefs that propagate through multi-agent workflows.
- **Limited availability of standardized AI-specific detection rule packs in SIEM platforms** — Standard SIEM platforms have limited support for detecting AI-specific attack patterns (e.g., prompt injection, multi-turn jailbreaks). Most AI-in-SIEM features focus on assisting investigation of traditional events rather than detecting attacks targeting AI systems.
- **Prompt injection dominates real incidents** — Adversa AI's July 2025 announcement of its 2025 AI Security Incidents Report says 35% of the real-world AI security incidents it collected were caused by simple prompts, with some leading to $100K+ in losses. GenAI was involved in 70% of incidents, but agentic AI caused the most dangerous failures, including crypto thefts, API abuse, legal problems, and supply chain attacks. The incident set is vendor-compiled, not a representative sample.
- **Indirect prompt injection is now observable in the wild** — CSA's April 26, 2026 research note reported that Google found a 32% relative increase in the malicious category of indirect prompt injection detections between November 2025 and February 2026 in Common Crawl snapshots of 2-3 billion pages each (English-language web, excluding most social media; Google described the sophistication as low), while Unit 42 documented real web-based IDPI payloads for SEO poisoning, ad-review evasion, data destruction, unauthorized transactions, sensitive information leakage, and system-prompt leakage. Monitoring needs prompt-source provenance, rendered-vs-raw content inspection, and intent analysis rather than simple keyword signatures.
- **CI/CD agents expose secrets through ordinary collaboration text** — Microsoft's [June 5, 2026 disclosure](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/06/05/securing-ci-cd-in-agentic-world-claude-code-github-action-case/) showed that untrusted issue, pull-request, and comment text could steer Claude Code GitHub Action's in-process Read tool to `/proc/self/environ`, outside the subprocess environment scrubber. Altering the credential before output also bypassed secret-pattern redaction. Anthropic blocked sensitive `/proc` paths in version 2.1.128, but the monitoring lesson is broader: preserve the originating GitHub event, tool authorization, file-read target, output transformation, and egress or comment-write event in one trace.
- **Monitoring as an attack target** — Since LLM-based monitoring inherits the same vulnerabilities it aims to detect, an attacker who compromises monitoring could blind the organization to ongoing attacks or create false alerts masking real threats.
- **AI-specific SIEM rules emerging but immature** — A community-built MITRE ATLAS app on Splunkbase (10 saved-search detection rules, disabled by default; a standalone community release, not a Splunk product) is an early example of AI attack detection rules in a SIEM. ANY.RUN's December 2025 release notes announced AI-generated Sigma rules; these are general threat-detection rules, not rules for attacks on AI systems. SigmaGen builds ATT&CK-mapped rules using LLMs. However, this review found no major SIEM vendor shipping a comprehensive AI-attack-specific rule pack — the gap between "AI assists SOC analysts" and "SIEM detects attacks on AI" persists. RSAC 2026 announcements (March 2026) narrowed but did not close this gap: Cisco announced six agents for Splunk Enterprise Security (Detection Builder, Triage, Guided Response, SOP, Malware Threat Reversing, and Automation Builder) with staggered availability through June 2026, and Datadog's Bits AI Security Analyst (now GA) handles alert investigation inside Cloud SIEM.
- **LLM routing-layer compromise** — Help Net Security (April 16, 2026) detailed research (arXiv 2604.08407) on 28 paid and 400 free LLM routers: 9 routers (1 paid, 8 free) actively injected malicious code into tool calls; one drained ETH from a researcher-owned private key; 17 routers touched researcher-owned AWS canary credentials. Two routers applied tampering conditionally — one only after a 50-request warm-up, another only for sessions that appeared to be running in autonomous "YOLO mode" on Rust or Go projects — which can keep short evaluations and test harnesses clean. Critically: no widely deployed mechanism verifies that the tool-call payload received by the client matches what the model originally produced. Client-side defenses (fail-closed policy gates, response-side anomaly screening, append-only transparency logs) remain DIY.
- **AI supply-chain pipeline compromise** — On March 24, 2026 malicious LiteLLM releases (v1.82.7, v1.82.8) went live on PyPI. LiteLLM's advisory says they were live from 10:39 UTC for about 40 minutes before quarantine and treats installs through 16:00 UTC as potentially affected; Wiz gives roughly 08:30-11:25 UTC and Trend Micro says PyPI yanked them about three hours after the first community reports (an unresolved source conflict). The packages dropped a `litellm_init.pth` loader (and an injected `litellm/proxy/proxy_server.py` payload in 1.82.7) that harvested environment variables, SSH keys, cloud credentials, Kubernetes data, Docker configs, shell history, database credentials, wallet files, and CI/CD secrets, AES-256/RSA-4096 encrypted them, and exfiltrated to `models.litellm[.]cloud` with header `X-Filename: tpcp.tar.gz`. Persistence was installed via `~/.config/sysmon/sysmon.py` and `~/.config/systemd/user/sysmon.service`, with a follow-on beacon to `https://checkmarx[.]zone/raw` writing `/tmp/pglog` and `/tmp/.pg_state`; where service-account tokens were available, privileged `node-setup-*` pods spread the payload across Kubernetes. The incident is stage 4 of a TeamPCP cascade (Trivy v0.69.4 on March 19, an npm worm across 44+ packages March 20-22, Checkmarx KICS / OpenVSX March 23, Telnyx PyPI 4.87.1/4.87.2 backdoored March 27). LiteLLM reports the official LiteLLM Proxy Docker image, which pins dependencies in `requirements.txt`, was not affected; pip installs during the window must be treated as full-credential exposure events, not just package removal. Monitoring lessons: package-install telemetry, outbound connections to the exfiltration and C2 domains (`models.litellm[.]cloud`, `checkmarx[.]zone`; the later Telnyx payload fetched from `83.142.209[.]203:8080`), `*.pth` file creation in `site-packages`, and unexplained `node-setup-*` pods all remain undervalued controls for AI-gateway supply chains.
- **Prompt-to-shell RCE in agent frameworks** — Microsoft Security Research's [May 7, 2026 disclosure](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/05/07/prompts-become-shells-rce-vulnerabilities-ai-agent-frameworks/) showed that prompt injection now compounds into native code execution when AI agent frameworks expose unsafe primitives. [CVE-2026-26030](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-26030) (Semantic Kernel Python < 1.39.4) used `eval()` inside a default in-memory vector store filter, letting attacker-controlled prompts traverse AST and class hierarchies to RCE. [CVE-2026-25592](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-25592) (Semantic Kernel .NET < 1.71.0) exposed `DownloadFileAsync` as a `[KernelFunction]` without path validation, enabling arbitrary file writes — including startup folder persistence — directly from model invocations. The defensive guidance is explicit: "your LLM is not a security boundary." Detection moves to **host-level** telemetry around agent processes (suspicious `cmd.exe`/`powershell.exe`/`bash.exe`/`curl.exe`/`certutil.exe` children, file writes to startup or scheduled-task paths, sandbox-to-host file movement), via Defender for Endpoint `DeviceProcessEvents` hunts on Semantic Kernel parent processes.
- **Telemetry manipulation as a first-class attack** — A [May 20, 2026 Help Net Security analysis](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/20/agentic-ai-security-llm-research/) of agentic AI in production operations elevated four overlapping attack categories: prompt injection via operational artifacts (tickets, wiki pages), retrieval poisoning of runbooks and incident histories, retrieval jamming that floods the knowledge base with blocker documents to trigger refusal loops, and telemetry manipulation where attackers shape the metrics and logs an agent reads to steer mitigation decisions without ever touching the model. The recommended architecture is propose-commit: models draft proposals only, non-bypassable policy-as-code gates enforce approvals, and integrity-protected audit logs let post-incident forensics reconstruct what the agent considered, which tools it called, what data it accessed, and why it chose its path. Monitoring metrics shift toward tool-call traces, gate-violation rates, refusal rates under jamming, and rollback completeness — not just latency or token counts.

- **LLM agents now run the intrusion itself** — Sysdig TRT's [May 10, 2026 observation](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/ai-agent-at-the-wheel-how-an-attacker-used-llms-to-move-from-a-cve-to-an-internal-database-in-4-pivots) describes what Sysdig TRT calls the first AI-agent-driven intrusion it has captured, an in-the-wild case it assesses from command-stream behavior as an LLM agent driving post-exploitation: Marimo notebook RCE (CVE-2026-39987) → credential harvest → AWS Secrets Manager replay through a Cloudflare Workers egress pool (12 redundant `GetSecretValue` calls across 11 edge IPs in 22 seconds, defeating IP-based detection) → SSH bastion → full PostgreSQL exfiltration in under two minutes. Sysdig points to four command-telemetry signatures (an inference, not direct evidence of an LLM): improvised targeting (dumping a `credential` table that didn't exist in the inferred schema), a Chinese-language planning comment leaked mid-execution at sub-second cadence across six worker IPs, machine-consumption formatting (`echo '---'` delimiters, quoted-EOF HEREDOCs bundling six SQL statements, `| head -30`, `2>/dev/null`), and output-driven handoffs reusing values from earlier command output. The monitoring lesson is new: **command-stream stylometry is now detection telemetry** — formatting choices that mark output as destined for LLM parsing rather than human eyes are alertable signal.
- **N-day weaponization of AI inference infrastructure in hours** — [Sysdig's LMDeploy analysis](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/cve-2026-33626-how-attackers-exploited-lmdeploy-llm-inference-engines-in-12-hours) caught CVE-2026-33626 (SSRF in LMDeploy's vision-model image URL loader, fixed in v0.12.3) being exploited against a Sysdig honeypot 12 hours 31 minutes after the GitHub advisory published — an 8-minute session probing AWS IMDS, Redis, MySQL, and LMDeploy's `/distserve/p2p_drop_connect` admin endpoint via out-of-band DNS callbacks. Vision-language endpoints that fetch attacker-supplied image URLs are SSRF primitives by design; Falco's instance-metadata-contact rules and egress monitoring on inference processes (link-local, RFC 1918, loopback destinations) are the practical detection layer.
- **The agent dissolves localhost trust boundaries** — Microsoft's [AutoJack research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/06/18/autojack-single-page-rce-host-running-ai-agent/) chained a browsing agent, an unauthenticated local MCP WebSocket, and attacker-controlled process arguments into host command execution on an unreleased AutoGen Studio development surface. The chain was fixed before a PyPI release, but it provides a concrete negative test for C12.2 and C12.4: alert when browser-capable agent processes reach privileged loopback control planes, when Python or Node agent parents spawn unexpected shells or download tools, and when high-risk actions lack an authenticated approval record.
- **Monitoring must be measured as a security control** — Google DeepMind's [AI Control Roadmap](https://deepmind.google/blog/securing-the-future-of-ai-agents/) reports an internal asynchronous monitor evaluated across one million coding-agent tasks and a live monitor for Gemini Spark. It measures coverage, recall, and time-to-response, and shifts from delayed review to synchronous blocking as action impact rises. It also warns that visible reasoning becomes a weaker signal when an agent can recognize or evade oversight, so C12.4 verification should exercise action and outcome telemetry rather than relying only on chain-of-thought inspection.
- **Persistent memory moves attacks across sessions** — Microsoft's [June 22, 2026 memory-security research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/06/22/guarding-ai-memory/) explains how poisoned memory can influence later tool selection, refusal behavior, or data disclosure after the originating interaction has disappeared from view. Its [implementation pattern](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/zero-trust/sfi/manage-agentic-memory-safety) treats memory as both high-value data and a behavioral control plane: log every create, read, update, and delete with identity, timestamp, source, and provenance; track propagation; retain history for rollback; and test multi-turn poisoning, delayed tool invocation, cross-context leakage, and payload assembly. Prompt-level alerts alone cannot reconstruct this delayed causal chain.

## Tooling & Implementation

Current tools, frameworks, and libraries that help implement these controls:

- **LLM observability:** [LangSmith](https://smith.langchain.com/) (Plus plan $39/seat/month plus usage), [Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/) (open-source MIT, self-hostable, prompt versioning, agent graph visualization), [Arize Phoenix](https://arize.com/) (open-source EL2, embedding drift monitoring, LLM-as-judge), [MLflow Tracing](https://mlflow.org/docs/latest/genai/tracing/) (OpenTelemetry-compatible tracing for LLM and agent calls, with production traces feeding evaluation datasets), [W&B Weave](https://docs.wandb.ai/weave/concepts/what-is-weave) (LLM traces, prompt/data/model versioning, production guardrails and scorers), [WhyLabs](https://whylabs.ai/) (the company has discontinued operations; its platform, whylogs, and langkit were released as open source), [Helicone](https://www.helicone.ai/) (gateway-based cost tracking)
- **OpenTelemetry GenAI SIG:** [Semantic conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/) (Development status; as of September 2026 the old specification pages say the GenAI conventions moved to the standalone [semantic-conventions-genai](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai) repository and are no longer maintained there) define attributes including `gen_ai.operation.name`, `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `gen_ai.tool.name`, provider-specific conventions for Anthropic/OpenAI/AWS Bedrock. Prompt content is opt-in/sensitive by design. As of April 28, 2026, [semantic-conventions v1.41.0](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions/releases/tag/v1.41.0) introduced a breaking `execute_tool` span-naming change requiring the tool name, so teams should pin schema versions and test downstream SIEM/APM parsers before upgrading. [Agent span conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans/) define create-agent, invoke-agent, invoke-workflow, and execute-tool span types with `gen_ai.conversation.id` tracking. Opt-in via `OTEL_SEMCONV_STABILITY_OPT_IN=gen_ai_latest_experimental`. Datadog [natively supports](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/llm-otel-semantic-convention/) OTel GenAI conventions for LLM observability. The project's 2026 ["Inside the LLM Call" walkthrough](https://opentelemetry.io/blog/2026/genai-observability/) demonstrates the three-span pattern (`invoke_agent` → `chat` → `execute_tool`), confirms content capture stays metadata-only unless explicitly enabled, and notes that VS Code Copilot, OpenAI Codex, and Claude Code now emit OTel telemetry — meaning coding-agent activity can land in the same trace pipeline as application LLM calls.
- **Drift detection:** [Evidently AI](https://www.evidentlyai.com/) (open-source drift detection, now supports LLM evaluations), [NannyML](https://www.nannyml.com/) (performance estimation without ground truth), WhyLabs (embedding drift for LLMs; open source after the company wound down). Key gap: LLM drift requires embedding drift + LLM-as-judge, not just statistical distribution shifts.
- **Grounding and hallucination evaluation:** NIST's [Building Evaluation Probes into Agentic AI](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/building-evaluation-probes-agentic-ai) project and [open-source demonstrator](https://github.com/usnistgov/agentic-research-evaluation-probes) provide a concrete C12.3.2/C12.3.3 pattern: test claims against a trusted corpus for faithfulness, completeness, and sufficiency, then retain the verdicts and rationales in a structured audit trail. This is research infrastructure, not a validated detector; NIST notes that the probes, rubrics, and model-based judges need further validation, so production use requires a human-labeled calibration set and continuous false-positive and false-negative measurement.
- **SIEM integration:** [Datadog LLM Observability](https://www.datadoghq.com/) (LiteLLM integration, out-of-the-box Cloud SIEM detection rules (general, not AI-specific), native OTel GenAI convention support; [Bits AI Security Analyst](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/bits-ai-security-analyst/) GA, Cloud SIEM-resident alert investigator), [Microsoft Sentinel](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/products/microsoft-sentinel) (RSAC 2026: [MCP entity analyzer GA](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoftsentinelblog/what%E2%80%99s-new-in-microsoft-sentinel-rsac-2026/4503971) for reasoned risk assessments, A365 Observability connector preview for AI agent telemetry, graph tools for relationship-based hunting), Splunk ([Cisco-announced agents](https://newsroom.cisco.com/c/r/newsroom/en/us/a/y2026/m03/cisco-reimagines-security-for-the-agentic-workforce.html) at RSAC 2026 for triage/detection/response; Cisco's [Galileo](https://blogs.cisco.com/news/cisco-announces-the-intent-to-acquire-galileo) acquisition (announced Apr 9, completed May 22, 2026) adds agent development lifecycle observability), Elastic. [MITRE ATLAS Splunk App](https://splunkbase.splunk.com/app/8527) — a community-built app with 10 saved-search AI attack detection rules in two tiers (not a Splunk product). [Sigma](https://github.com/SigmaHQ/sigma) open standard enables SIEM-agnostic detection rules convertible to 40+ platforms; [SigmaGen](https://blogs.night-wolf.io/sigmagen-ai-powered-attck-mapped-threat-detection-with-sigma-rules) automates ATT&CK-mapped rule generation.
- **Prompt injection detection:** [Lakera Guard](https://www.lakera.ai/) (vendor-reported sub-50 ms runtime latency and 100+ languages; now part of Check Point), [LLM Guard](https://github.com/protectai/llm-guard) (open-source input/output validation), [Rebuff](https://github.com/protectai/rebuff) (4-layer self-hardening detection). Microsoft's [March 2026 prompt-abuse playbook](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/03/12/detecting-analyzing-prompt-abuse-in-ai-tools/) recommends capturing full URL context (including fragments after `#`), input-sanitization failures, and anomalous output patterns; correlating AI interactions with file-access audit trails surfaces extractive abuse and indirect injection that per-request guards miss. The [CSA April 2026 note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-indirect-prompt-injection-in-the-wild-2026/) and [Unit 42 IDPI telemetry](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/ai-agent-prompt-injection/) make a stronger case for browser/web-crawler-side detection that classifies raw, rendered, and hidden content before an agent ingests it.
- **Inference-host runtime detection:** The Sysdig LMDeploy and Marimo cases make a concrete tooling argument: [Falco](https://falco.org/) rules for instance-metadata-service contact from containers/hosts ("Contact EC2/GCP/Azure Instance Metadata Service") plus egress monitoring on inference processes (alerting on connections to link-local, RFC 1918, or loopback destinations) catch SSRF-to-IMDS chains that LLM-layer observability never sees. For LLM-agent-driven intrusions, Sysdig recommends alerting on credential-file access from notebook environments, cloud API calls fanned across multiple edge-network IPs within seconds, schema enumeration followed by rapid table dumps, and command formatting that targets machine consumption (delimiter echoes, HEREDOC bundling, output suppression) in SSH sessions.
- **Agent runtime and CI/CD hardening:** Microsoft's [Claude Code GitHub Action case](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/06/05/securing-ci-cd-in-agentic-world-claude-code-github-action-case/) shows that shell sandboxing is insufficient when an in-process file tool retains access to runner secrets; record and correlate the untrusted event, tool authorization, file target, returned data, transformations, and every output channel. The [AutoJack disclosure](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/06/18/autojack-single-page-rce-host-running-ai-agent/) adds concrete host and network hunts: unexpected shell or downloader children of Python/Node agent processes, WebSocket traffic from browser automation to privileged loopback endpoints, and non-corporate navigation during an agent session. The affected AutoGen Studio development surface was fixed before a PyPI release, but the detection pattern applies to other browser-plus-local-control-plane designs.
- **Propose-commit and gate telemetry:** The [Help Net Security May 20, 2026 survey](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/20/agentic-ai-security-llm-research/) argues that production agents need a strict propose-commit split — models draft proposals, non-bypassable policy-as-code gates enforce approvals and rollback, and audit logs are integrity-protected so post-incident reconstruction is possible. The corresponding monitoring metrics — tool-call traces, gate-violation rates, refusal rates under retrieval-jamming, and rollback completeness — are still custom dashboards in most deployments rather than shipped features in observability platforms.
- **LLM routing-layer defenses:** The router study (arXiv 2604.08407) notes that no provider enforces cryptographic integrity between client and upstream model, and [Help Net Security's coverage](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/16/llm-router-security-risk-agent-commands/) points to client-side defenses, a DIY stack: client-side fail-closed policy gates, response-side anomaly screening that diffs the router's tool-call payload against the model's original output, append-only transparency logs that let downstream auditors reconstruct the request chain, and high-risk shell command blocklists before tool execution. This review did not find a tool that implements end-to-end router-payload integrity verification, but the search was not exhaustive.
- **Agent monitoring:** [AgentOps](https://www.agentops.ai/) (session replay, time-travel debugging, 400+ LLMs and frameworks), [Lunary](https://lunary.ai/) (conversation replay, topic classification), Weights & Biases Weave (hierarchical multi-agent tracing), [AG2](https://docs.ag2.ai/latest/docs/blog/2026/02/08/AG2-OpenTelemetry-Tracing/) (Feb 2026, native OTel tracing for multi-agent systems — structured hierarchical traces of conversations, agent turns, tool executions across local and distributed deployments), [OpenObserve](https://openobserve.ai/blog/product-update-march-2026/) (Mar 2026, v0.70.0 — LLM observability with DAG trace visualization, session-level multi-turn inspection, per-user token/latency analytics), [Arize AX](https://arize.com/blog/best-ai-observability-tools-for-autonomous-agents-in-2026/) (Agent Graph visualization renders execution as a tree rather than linear trace — shows sub-agent delegations, tool fires, and state changes; built on Phoenix + OpenInference), [Galileo](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/cisco-to-acquire-galileo-ai-agent-observability-cant-run-at-human-speed/) (Cisco acquisition completed May 22, 2026; hallucination/bias detection, real-time multi-agent guardrails, cost + security metrics across the Agent Development Lifecycle)
- **Training-data & model-lifecycle audit (C12.5):** [MLflow Model Registry](https://mlflow.org/docs/latest/model-registry/) records model versions and lineage from experiment to deployment. [OpenLineage](https://openlineage.io/) paired with [Marquez](https://marquezproject.ai/) captures dataset and transformation lineage events for C12.5.1, while [DVC](https://dvc.org/) provides content-addressed dataset versions that help reproduce those records. Labeling platforms must separately emit actor, timestamp, item, prior value, and new value for C12.5.2. C12.5.3 needs an append-only record for every model change, and C12.5.4 needs source, writer identity, and timestamp attached when each document is ingested. The gap is a unified tamper-evident audit trail: most teams still join a registry, lineage service, labeling log, document metadata store, and immutable retention layer.
- **Log integrity and forensics:** Hash chaining (each log entry references previous entry's hash), cryptographic digital signatures per agent identity, append-only immutable storage with object locking and retention policies. Identity-bound logging ties each entry to agent identifier, tenant context, authorization scope, and delegation status. Automated tamper detection monitors for missing sequences, invalid hash chains, or signature verification failures. See [LoginRadius guide](https://www.loginradius.com/blog/engineering/ensure-log-integrity-non-repudiation-ai-agents) for implementation patterns.
- **Incident response:** [CoSAI AI IR Framework](https://www.coalitionforsecureai.org/defending-ai-systems-a-new-framework-for-incident-response-in-the-age-of-intelligent-technology/) (2025 — CACAO-standard playbooks for AI-specific attacks), PagerDuty, Opsgenie with AI-specific runbooks. Gap: no widely adopted AI-specific IR tools yet.
- **DAG/workflow visualization:** [LangGraph Studio](https://docs.langchain.com/langgraph-platform/langgraph-studio) (visual agent workflow debugging), [Apache Airflow 3.0](https://airflow.apache.org/) (April 2025, 30M+ monthly downloads, 30% for MLOps), [Prefect](https://www.prefect.io/), [Dagster](https://dagster.io/) (asset-centric, GA Components Oct 2025; Dagster is now part of Prefect)

## Open Research Questions

- [ ] **When should full prompt/response content be logged?** — C12.1 requires useful session, policy, inference, and retrieval telemetry but does not make full content a universal field. OTel GenAI treats message content as opt-in and sensitive, while content-level jailbreak and prompt-injection detection needs some form of inspection. Teams still need a defensible trigger, legal basis, redaction policy, access boundary, and retention period for any full-content capture.
- [ ] **Will OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions stabilize in time?** — Core GenAI conventions were in "Development" status at the original location (the pages now say they moved to a standalone repository), and semantic-conventions v1.41.0 introduced an April 2026 `execute_tool` span naming change. Agent spans now cover create-agent, invoke-agent, invoke-workflow, and execute-tool types, but the transition plan for stable status has not been defined. OTel production deployment doubled year-over-year to 11% (Elastic 2026), and 89% of respondents with OTel in production rate vendor OTel compliance critical or very important. Schema changes could still break existing instrumentation, though the `OTEL_SEMCONV_STABILITY_OPT_IN` mechanism provides a migration path.
- [ ] **How do you detect multi-turn abuse trajectories at scale?** — Crescendo and Deceptive Delight attacks can slip past per-request monitoring. A Scale AI study found multi-turn human jailbreaks exceeding 70% ASR on HarmBench against defenses reporting single-digit ASR for automated single-turn attacks. Session-level analysis requires correlating across requests — expensive and architecturally complex.
- [ ] **Can AI-specific SIEM detection rules become standard?** — MITRE ATLAS Splunk app was the first step, and Sigma/SigmaGen now enable SIEM-agnostic rule generation convertible to 40+ platforms. Microsoft Sentinel's MCP entity analyzer (GA at RSAC, Mar 2026) adds AI-powered risk assessment. But comprehensive AI-attack-specific rule packs remain absent from major SIEM vendors — the gap between "AI assists SOC analysts" and "SIEM detects attacks on AI" is narrowing but persists.
- [ ] **How do you distinguish adversarial drift from natural concept drift?** — Both manifest as degraded outputs. Adversarial drift may be intentional poisoning (PoisonedRAG, VIA). Current tools detect drift but don't attribute cause. CSA (Sep 2025) warns incidents are often misattributed to natural drift rather than active attack.
- [ ] **How should shadow AI be governed?** — Varonis found 98% of studied organizations have employees using unsanctioned apps, including shadow AI (apps generally, not AI-only). Agent-based shadow AI (MCP servers, OAuth tokens) creates elevated-privilege access chains invisible to IT. No standard governance framework exists.
- [ ] **Can monitoring systems protect themselves from the attacks they detect?** — LLM-based monitoring inherits the vulnerabilities it aims to detect. An attacker who compromises the monitoring layer via prompt injection or adversarial inputs could blind the organization to ongoing attacks or generate false alerts masking real threats. No standard architecture for self-protecting monitoring exists.
- [ ] **How should monitoring preserve prompt provenance across raw, rendered, and hidden content?** — CSA and Unit 42's April 2026 findings show attackers embedding indirect prompts in HTML attributes, hidden comments, CSS-suppressed text, multilingual payloads, and Base64-like encodings. Current logs often store only the final text sent to the model, not the source, rendering state, or trust boundary that explains why the agent treated attacker data as instructions.
- [ ] **How do you close the observability-implementation gap?** — Among Elastic's 2026 respondents already using GenAI for observability, 85% plan LLM observability but 8% have finished it. OTel production deployment doubled to 11% but remains low. The gap between intent and deployed monitoring creates a window where most AI systems run effectively unmonitored in production.
- [ ] **How do you detect cascading failures across multi-agent systems before downstream damage?** — OWASP ASI08 (2026) lists cascading failures, where one bad decision is amplified across agents. Current monitoring tools observe individual agents but lack cross-agent propagation detection, so cascading failures may complete their damage before detection fires.
- [ ] **Can anyone monitor integrity across the LLM routing layer?** — April 2026 research on 428 routers found 9 silently rewriting tool calls, with two using conditional triggers, one of them only for autonomous "YOLO mode" sessions — evading evaluation harnesses by design. No widely deployed mechanism verifies that the tool-call payload received by the client matches what the model originally produced. Open questions: Should routers be required to sign payloads? Should clients maintain a verifier that replays the model request through an independent path and diffs the tool call? What telemetry schema should downstream systems consume to reconstruct the full request chain?
- [ ] **Can SOC-speed AI agents outpace the AI agents they monitor?** — RSAC 2026 marked the transition from "AI-assisted SOC" to purpose-built agentic SOC (Splunk's six Enterprise Security agents, Datadog Bits AI Security Analyst, Cisco/Galileo). But the detection layer now inherits every risk it watches for: if a Splunk SOC agent can be prompt-injected via an alert field, the attacker inherits the SOC's privileges. No public work yet measures prompt-injection resilience of SOC agents against the attacks they are deployed to catch.
- [ ] **Can SDLC agents safely read untrusted collaboration text while holding build credentials?** — Comment and Control showed the conflict directly: AI coding agents must read PR and issue text to work, but the same runtime often holds API keys, GitHub tokens, and commit privileges. The open problem is not just input filtering; it is how to enforce hard separation between untrusted workflow text, agent tools, build secrets, comments, commits, and Actions logs.
- [ ] **How do you detect telemetry manipulation against the agent's own observation surface?** — The May 20, 2026 Help Net Security survey treats telemetry manipulation as a peer of prompt injection: attackers shape metrics, logs, runbooks, and ticket text to steer agent decisions without touching the model. Current SIEM and APM stacks were built to trust their own telemetry, not to detect when it has been weaponized. Open questions: should agent-consumed telemetry be cryptographically attested? Should retrieved runbooks carry provenance and integrity tags? And how do you distinguish "the agent was prompt-injected" from "the agent was fed manipulated metrics" in post-incident triage?
- [ ] **Can command-stream stylometry reliably identify LLM-agent intrusions?** — Sysdig's May 10, 2026 Marimo case surfaced four telltale signatures (improvised targeting, leaked planning comments, machine-consumption output formatting, output-driven value handoffs) — all observable in standard shell/SSH telemetry, and all clustered within a 113-second window. But these are one campaign's habits, not validated detection science: a careful operator can strip delimiters and suppress comments, and legitimate automation (including defensive AI agents) produces similar formatting. Open questions: which signatures survive adversarial adaptation, what false-positive rate machine-consumption heuristics carry in DevOps-heavy environments, and whether EDR or SIEM vendors will ship LLM-operator detections before the pattern becomes commonplace.
- [ ] **What is the right detection layer for prompt-to-shell RCE in agent SDKs?** — CVE-2026-26030 and CVE-2026-25592 show that vulnerabilities in agent-framework primitives (`eval()` in default filters, `[KernelFunction]`-exposed file write) collapse the gap between model output and host code execution. Microsoft's recommendation is host-level process and file-system telemetry around agent processes, but most LLM observability stacks today still focus on prompt, response, token, and tool-call traces inside the SDK. The open question is who owns this layer — EDR, AI observability, or a new agent-runtime category — and how SOC-resident AI agents are expected to consume it.
- [ ] **How do you contain and roll back poisoned memory after it propagates?** — Lifecycle CRUD events can show who changed a memory, when, and from which source, but shared memory and sub-agent copying can spread derived state beyond the original record. The open question is how an interoperable provenance graph and rollback mechanism would identify every affected agent, tenant, cache, tool decision, and downstream write without crossing isolation or privacy boundaries.

- [EU AI Act Article 12](https://www.euaiact.com/article/12) — High-risk AI must technically allow automatic logging over system lifetime. [Article 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/) — Deployers must retain logs at least 6 months. [Article 19](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/19/) — Providers must retain automatically generated logs. **Timeline update:** the [Digital Omnibus provisional agreement](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/) (May 7, 2026) defers Annex III high-risk obligations from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027 and Annex I (product-regulated) from August 2027 to August 2028, with an Article 50(2) transition to December 2, 2026 for systems already on the market before August 2, 2026. The Commission's [AI Act timeline](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/timeline/timeline-implementation-eu-ai-act) shows these dates as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, which was published in the Official Journal on July 24, 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and entered into force on July 27, 2026; the May 2026 analysis is at [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/eu-ai-act-update-timeline-relief-targeted-simplification-and-new-prohibitions/)).
- [ISO/IEC 42001 A.6.2.8](https://www.isms.online/iso-42001/annex-a-controls/a-6-ai-system-life-cycle/a-6-2-8-ai-system-recording-of-event-logs/) — AI event logs must be tamper-evident, lifecycle-mapped, and fully auditable. Records consequential decisions, anomalies, policy overrides, retraining events.
- [NIST IR 8596 — Cyber AI Profile](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8596/iprd) (preliminary draft, Dec 2025; comment period closed Jan 2026; Spring 2026 working sessions held April-May 2026) — DETECT section covers AI-specific detection; RESPOND covers AI incident response. Its logging considerations say logs should show request data, response data, and whether responses matched policy, and suggest protecting logs from tampering (no retention period is given in the preliminary draft). [NIST SP 800-61r3](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final) provides the CSF 2.0-aligned incident response profile that IR 8596 AI-specific guidance builds upon. The parallel [NIST AI Agent Standards Initiative](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence/ai-agent-standards-initiative) (announced Feb 17, 2026; page last updated Aug 14, 2026) is developing voluntary guidance around identity, authorization, security evaluations, and secure human-agent and multi-agent interactions.
- [NIST AI 100-2e2025 — Adversarial Machine Learning](https://www.nist.gov/publications/adversarial-machine-learning-taxonomy-and-terminology-attacks-and-mitigations-0) — taxonomy and terminology for data poisoning, evasion, privacy breach, abuse, and mitigation methods; useful for classifying C12.2 alerts and investigating unexplained shifts under C12.3.
- [NIST AI 800-4 — Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.800-4.pdf) (Mar 2026) — separates functionality, operational, human-factors, security, compliance, and large-scale-impact monitoring. Its documented barriers map directly to C12 implementation: fragmented logging across distributed infrastructure, privacy-versus-granularity tradeoffs, use-case-specific metrics, and the lack of trusted monitoring methods and standards.
- [ISO/IEC 42005:2025](https://www.iso.org/standard/42005) — AI System Impact Assessment (published May 28, 2025). Guidance standard that requires recording methodologies, findings, mitigation strategies, and ongoing monitoring plans across the AI lifecycle. Annex B maps the relationship to [ISO/IEC 23894:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/77304.html) (AI risk management).
- [CoSAI AI IR Framework](https://www.coalitionforsecureai.org/defending-ai-systems-a-new-framework-for-incident-response-in-the-age-of-intelligent-technology/) (2025) — CACAO-standard playbooks for AI-specific attacks
- [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2026](https://github.com/GenAI-Security-Project/GenAI-LLM-Top10/tree/main/2026/final) — LLM01 (log prompt patterns), LLM02 (monitor for PII/secrets leakage), LLM03 (audit tool calls), LLM08 (monitor hidden-context extraction), LLM06 (token usage monitoring)
- [OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026)](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/) — ASI08 (Cascading Failures) requires monitoring propagation across agent networks; ASI01 (Goal Hijack) and ASI03 (Identity & Privilege Abuse) demand continuous behavioral monitoring
- [OWASP AI Security Solutions Landscape for Agentic AI Q2 2026](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/ai-security-solutions-landscape-for-agentic-ai-q2-2026/) (Mar 17, 2026, updated quarterly) — maps open-source and commercial solutions across the agentic AI lifecycle against the Agentic Threats and Mitigations guide and SecOps duties; useful for shortlisting monitoring/detection tooling per lifecycle stage
- [CSA AI Controls Matrix](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/ai-controls-matrix) (Jul 2025) — 243 controls across 18 domains including Logging and Monitoring domain
- [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) — MEASURE and MANAGE functions address runtime monitoring and risk treatment
